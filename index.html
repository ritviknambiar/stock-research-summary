Learning from the Stock Market

What really moves stock prices, and what can honest research and reflection teach me about thinking more carefully?

My introduction to financial markets didn't begin in a classroom. It began through conversations with my father, who would explain how stock markets work and why prices change. What first looked like a world of numbers soon became a puzzle: why could a company's share price fall despite strong results, and why might investors react differently to the same economic news? I came to see that understanding markets meant understanding expectations, incentives and human behaviour, not just following prices.

Curiosity turned into exploration, and I started small. I did not trade myself and I do not have a trading account. Instead, I researched companies and shared my analysis with my father as one input he could consider; every investment decision was his own. To organise this, I built an Excel evaluation tracker to follow market trends, record outcomes and look for patterns. Along the way I became interested in how expectations about future growth, interest rates and uncertainty shape valuations. Most importantly, I learned to question what the numbers appeared to suggest rather than accepting them at face value.

What I set out to research

I wanted to understand what really moves stock prices, and to learn from real outcomes rather than from theory alone. My goal was never to beat the market. It was to become a more careful, more honest analyst, so that the research I shared with my father was better reasoned each time.

The question I kept coming back to was: when a stock went up or down, was it because my reasoning was right, or because of something else?

How I did it

StepWhat I did
1. ChooseBuilt a focused list of 42 companies across 5 sectors (Railways, Technology, Mining & Metals, Banking, Manufacturing), with a wider watchlist of 103 for future ideas.
2. TrackRecorded each stock's price and 52-week high and low in my Excel evaluation tracker. I used the midpoint of that range as a simple “fair price” marker to judge whether a stock looked cheap or expensive compared with its recent past.
3. ShareShared my research with my father for discussion. Over about six months in 2024 he made 19 trades in his own account, deciding for himself whether and how to use my research. I recorded each buy price, sell price and sale date for my analysis.
4. CompareMeasured each trade's return and used the Nifty 50, India's main market index, as a yardstick for how the market as a whole moved.
5. ReflectLooked back at every outcome to work out what actually moved the price, and what I would research differently next time.

How I evaluated the results

I used two lenses: one about money and one about reasoning.

1. How did it compare with the market? I compared each trade's return with the Nifty 50 over the same period. I looked at both the typical trade (the median) and the return on all the money invested, because a large position counts more in the second measure.

2. Was the idea right? For every outcome, I wrote down the original idea, what actually happened, and the main reason the price moved (company results, sector trend, company news, valuation, the wider economy, or hype). Then I placed each outcome in one of four boxes:

Good result
Weak result
Idea was right
A the research worked
B right idea, but timing or other forces hurt
Idea was wrong
C made money, but for the wrong reason
D a real research mistake

This stopped me from confusing “the trade made money” with “my analysis was good”.

What I found

Insight 1

Close to the market, but the money told a different story

The typical trade returned 13.4%, close to the Nifty 50's 12.7% over the same six months. But the return on all the money invested was 9.6%, behind the market.

Insight 2

One big position held everything back

Most trades used a small share of the money, but one stock took 39% of it and returned only 2.5%. Without that one position, the overall return would have been about 14%. Owning 19 stocks looked diversified, but the money was not.

Insight 3

Making money and being right are not the same

Nine outcomes were “A”: the idea was right and it paid off. But four were “C”: the stock rose because of rumours or hype, not my reasoning, and those trades earned about as much on average (21.0%) as the right-reason wins (20.7%). Six were real mistakes, including a loss on Lucid, a U.S. electric-vehicle company. I also noticed that four stocks kept rising strongly after they were sold.

My learnings

These are my working hypotheses from looking back at the results. They are personal reflections for my own learning, not recommendations for anyone else.

  1. Diversification is about the money, not just the number of stocks.In this data, keeping any single stock to a small share (around 10%) of the portfolio, and spread across sectors, would have stopped one result from dominating everything.
  2. A plan for falling prices matters.Deciding in advance at what level a falling stock gets re-checked (for example 15% below the buying price) is a common risk-management idea that I want to study further. Some holdings kept falling with no plan to act.
  3. The exit matters as much as the entry.Writing down a target price and a review date at the start would make outcomes easier to judge. Several stocks rose a lot after they were sold, and others fell sharply afterwards; a plan makes this less of a guess.
  4. A profit does not prove the research was right.It is important to ask why a stock moved. Gains from rumours or hype do not repeat reliably.
  5. Watch the whole sector, not just the company.Sector trends drove more of the results than any other single reason; whole groups of government-owned banks, power and railway stocks rose together.
  6. Write the reasoning down first.A written reason makes it possible to judge honestly afterwards, and protects against remembering being more right than I was.

Turning my learnings into a simple formula

To make my research more consistent, I turned these learnings into a simple checklist score and a few rules in my Excel evaluation tracker. It is a learning exercise: it does not predict the future, has not been tested, and is not a recommendation to buy or sell anything. It helps me decide which ideas deserve more research.

Step 2: The rules

Gap to mid-range marker = (Mid-range marker − Current price) ÷ Current price
Illustrative weight (hypothetical) = (Idea Score ÷ 10) × 10% of a sample portfolio
Review level = Buying price × 0.85 (re-check the research if the price falls 15%)
Decision
Score 7 or more → share the research with my father for discussion 4 to 6 → keep researching 3 or less → drop the idea

Hypothetical example (not a real stock). A stock trades at 80, with a 52-week high of 120 and low of 60. Its mid-range marker is (120 + 60) ÷ 2 = 90, so the gap to the marker is (90 − 80) ÷ 80 = 12.5%. If it scores 8 out of 10, the illustrative weight in a sample portfolio would be 8%, with a review level at 80 × 0.85 = 68 and a review date six months out. This only shows how the arithmetic works.

My next step is to test, on paper, how well this score sorts well-researched ideas from weak ones.

The big takeaway

The biggest lesson for me is that good investing is less about finding one winning stock and more about good habits: spreading risk, planning exits, and being honest about whether a win came from skill or luck. What started as conversations with my dad became a way of thinking: look past the numbers, ask why, and learn from every outcome.
